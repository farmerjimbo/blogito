vi. a different Other: on Doppelganger and postcolonialism

February 9, 2026 · James Cody Phelan

A Different Other: On Doppelganger and Postcolonialism James Cody Phelan

Naomi Klein’s 2023 book Doppelganger is about many things. The book hits on themes of identity, media, politics, disease, gender, race, obsession, etc. In so many words, though, the book is about being about all those things. It is self-referential as a book about “our times” as diagnosed by Klein. Doppelganger uses Klein’s relationship with her own doppelganger, the writer and pundit Naomi Wolf, as a literary tool to decipher the great many forces shaping our socio-political landscape, and to explain how that landscape creates a destabilized, fractured reality. Wolf is Klein’s turnkey so that she might pop open the zeitgeist’s hood, rummage through the internal machinery, and report back on what she’s found. The project is holistic in that sense. Klein wants to prod the discursive components of our culture to pinpoint exactly where they all connect. That is Doppelganger’s great ambition: Find the center of the storm. Klein is not necessarily interested in explaining where the storm comes from or why it exists, but she does seek to highlight how the competing currents of modernity interact, clash, and contradict—often irrationally.

The branching themes and narratives of Doppelganger collide near the end of the book in Chapter 14: The Unshakable Ethnic Double. Here, Klein looks to thread together the previous 300 pages into one cohesive framework for thinking about the world in 2023. By this point she’s established terminology to help her toward this goal. The “Mirror World” is her shorthand for the alternative pseudo-reality inhabited by figures like Naomi Wolf, Steve Banon, Q, and Alex Jones characterized by suspicion of global elites, conspiratorial thinking, anti-intellectualism, and hyper-capitalism. “Shadow Lands” are the overlooked depredated places and people on top of which our modern consumerist lives rely upon but refuse to acknowledge. And of course, the titular “doppelganger” serves as both a recurring analytical trope within the book and (increasingly as the book goes on) a conceptual descriptor of the results of contemporary society—here, suggesting that elements of reality are-and-are-also-not as they seem. In Klein’s estimation, certain things, ideas, and even states are doppelgangers, or at least exhibit doppelganger-ness. The theoretical scaffolding of the book builds on these different concepts to arrive at the end of the book, the crucial Chapter 14, with the language needed to connect the dots and approach the stickiest of all topics: that of Israel-Palestine. Klein contends that the state of Israel-Palestine is the ultimate manifestation of the book’s swirling thesis. Each element of her case—the Mirror World, Shadow Lands, and doppelgangers—reaches a level of knotted unreality that heightens the “complication” of the Middle East context. Israel-Palestine is a hall of mirrors in a book like Doppelganger, which is all about competing versions of truth. In the Holy Land, the gap between conspiracy theories and discernable systems of power blur beyond separation. Stereotypes are pushed to their absolute limits while reinforcing themselves in their repetition as “true.” Anti-Zionism endures as a legitimate political endeavor but is made hazy by the very real specter of anti-Semitism. The Shadow Lands exist in remarkably obvious areas, drawn on maps and physically walled off, marked as territories of Otherness by law. Identities in this place form at unusual, conflicting intersections of race, nationality, religion, language, and location. The divide between Israelis and Palestinians is not inherently based on skin color or religion, though the language of Orientalism has adopted those signifiers to calcify what is otherwise a political distinction. (“For what is a racial profile if not a doppelganger made by the state?” Klein asks (pg. 281-282)). In all these ways, Israel-Palestine functions in a quantum state of absolute doppelganger-ness; two states existing simultaneously within one location, two separate realities stacked on top of each other. Klein writes: The doppelganger nature of the country’s identity is embedded in the dualistic language used to describe it, in which everything is double and never singular: Israel-Palestine, Arab and Jew, Two States, The Conflict. Based on a fantasy of symmetrical power, this suturing together of two peoples implies conjoined twins and a state of unending struggle, an irresolvable sibling rivalry between the two peoples, both descended from Abraham. (Klein, pg. 299) This language closely echoes Edward Said’s Orientalism, in which Said describes the connection between the “Orient” and “Occident” as “two geographical entities thus support and to an extent reflect each other” (Said, pg. 5). The duality of Israel-Palestine is captured as a binary arising from Orientalist discourse, a point explicitly made by Said and suggested by Klein. However, here at the center of Doppelganger’s storm, the dichotomy manifests as a very literal place defined by those power dynamics. Klein references the Orientalist Othering that presupposes the unequal doubling of the two states, analyzing how an ideology can prop up an “Other” as a competing force while retaining discursive and material authority over that force. The Palestinians are, narratively, the great timeless enemy of Israel and the corralled, disempowered, feeble prisoners that cannot be seen or acknowledged. The Palestinians are-and-are-also-not. They are hidden. Buried into near non-existence. To this point, Klein quotes several figures, including the mid-century journalist I.F. Stone who wrote in 1967, “For the Zionist, the Arab was the Invisible Man,” and the former Israeli Prime Minister Golda Meir who famously said, “there was no such thing as Palestinians… They did not exist” (Klein, pg. 300). For what it is worth, my own experience in Israel, the same year as Doppelganger’s release, closely echoes this sentiment. Of the several hundred Israelis I met living at a Kibbutz in the Negev Desert, many of whom were sympathetic to the Palestinian plight, not one ever referred to a Palestinian as a ‘Palestinian.’ And the issue was not one of translation. They understood me perfectly and at length when referring to Palestinians but could call them nothing other than ‘Arabs.’ Many did not even have the language to refer to the Occupied West Bank by that title or any other, choosing to reference specific areas or cities within the West Bank by their Hebrew titles while never acknowledging the West Bank as a distinct territory (whether they considered it part of Israel or not). These lexical gaps are indicative of the role the Palestinians play within the psyche of the Israelis. Both ever-present and not. But how can a people be both the Other and Nothing? Only as a shadow, Klein argues. Only through their externally reinforced and rigidly internalized doppelganger-ness. As with all true doppelgangers, the double identity of Israel-Palestine is a falsity. A charade. The doppelganger, whether in a novel or in material world, is simply a conceptual trope meant to highlight the contradictions within a destabilized subject. The doppelganger reflects the darkest characteristics of the original. The portrait in The Picture of Dorian Gray, for example, bares Gray’s petty solipsism. The unrestrained Mr. Hyde unburdens Dr. Jekyll’s hate and desire. So too does the shadow of Palestine reveal and upend the truth of Israel: Like all segregated societies that are layered on top of each other, Israel and Palestine are not two distinct geographies. Instead, they make up a singular doppelganger society, requiring a doubling of everything: schools, roads, laws, courts. It’s a psychological prison for Jewish Israelis, locked into a fortress of fear and denial, and it’s a very literal prison for Palestinians, entrapped in warren of walls and checkpoints in the West Bank, in the open air prison that is Gaza, and in the sprawling jail cells that have made incarceration such a routinized part of daily life… (Klein, pg. 303-304) Klein’s framing helps explain how the state of Israel operates as both victim and perpetrator; victim of their own form of Orientalism—anti-Semitism—while enacting and relying upon Orientalism to discursively eradicate the Palestinian. This point is of particular importance; one I want to make explicitly clear, for the nuance of Doppelganger’s success as a text (and this essay) depends on this conclusion. The mirroring of Orientalism and anti-Semitism cannot be overlooked in this context, as both Klein and Said are quick to point out, BUT the “opposite” narratives of the two discursive Otherings do not cancel one another out, nor do they abstract each other into permanent historical truth. That Orientalism (or, perhaps more clearly, anti-Arabism) and anti-Semitism exist in the same place at the same time does not reinforce a perpetual, equal antagonism. They both exert power, at times in relation to one another, yes, but they are independent social constructions that constitute different dynamics of power. Both are real and contemporaneous. Both are applied onto their subject. And in both, the subjects are not free to define themselves. However, the conflation of Orientalism and anti-Semitism represents an unlike attribution of discursive power, particularly in Israel-Palestine, wherein one group so clearly controls the other’s ability to speak. It’s a tragic arrangement. That the two “resemble each other very closely,” Said writes, “is a historical, cultural, and political truth that needs only to be mentioned to an Arab Palestinian for its irony to be perfectly understood (Said, pg. 27). Israeli Jews are routinely made the object of anti-Semitic discourse—made to be an object by “civilizing” forces—and as a response, cordon off a definition of self that precludes a different Other. Here, we return to the Mirror World and the greater project of Doppelganger I started this essay with. The book does explore some of the origins of anti-Semitism, but Klein, again, is not especially interested in deconstructing where prejudice arises or how prejudice differs between contexts. Instead, she identifies how prejudice reflects itself across the boundary of the Mirror World, how it replicates and mutates. Klein argues how the splitting of reality—whether through political borders or media ecosystems—further disturbs who we are to one another, hiding some things, making other things more grossly visible. “At bottom,” she summarizes, “it comes down to who and what we cannot bear to see—in our past, in our present, and in the future racing toward us” (Klein, pg. 322). The manifold forces working on and through us only accelerate that disruption, confuse us further, and destabilize our sense of truth. Since anti-Semitism and Orientalism and every other form of discursive power arrangement inflect upon us continuously and with growing rapidity, it only makes sense that some of them catch the looking glass at an odd angle and refract into new, bizarre directions. Klein makes this case, as usual, in reference to Naomi Wolf and her personal transition into the Mirror World. In 2014, Wolf made a series of criticisms against the state of Israel for their assault on Gaza that killed over 1,400 Palestinian civilians. For this criticism, Wolf, an American Jew, experienced a barrage of harassment and professional repercussions, eventually losing her teaching job at Barnard. Klein questions whether this event might have caused Wolf’s descent: Having run afoul of many of the same guardians of official Jewry over the past three decades, I have no doubt that these events were both frightening and painful for Wolf. These were her friends, family members, and colleagues turning on her. Zionism, moreover, had always been a plank in our political home. Now that plank was giving way. Which raises a possible question: Did that experience—followed by the public excommunications—contribute to how unmoored she became in subsequent years? Does losing her political home partially explain how far she would stray to find a new home?" (Klein, pg. 307). I would be hard pressed to find an example that better encapsulates the competing, contradicting identities and power dynamics that drive Doppelganger toward the end of its spinning conclusion. Klein, a white Canadian Jewish woman, writes about how Wolf, a white American Jewish woman, was ostracized by Jewish elites for criticizing Israel for killing Palestinians. Both writers play a game of ‘positionality’ musical chairs and end up stumbling. Wolf spins out from the experience, destabilized about her positionality, perhaps justifiably so, and begins pedaling other untruths about vaccines, gender essentialism, and all manner of charged topics. Klein spends years being mistaken for Wolf wondering how her own identity runs counter or parallel to her doppelganger. Then, in Doppelganger, Klein runs Wolf through the ringer, grilling every angle of her new politics, before ending on a more conciliatory note. By the final pages of the book, Klein finds a great deal of empathy for her American look-a-like. Wolf’s story is recognizable to her. Almost reasonable. She can understand how Wolf struggles to find her feet during a deeply uncertain period. Abandoned by the orientation of Zionism, launched into a frothy sea of personalized social media, cut from physical interaction by a global pandemic, betrayed by third-wave feminism, and asked to appreciate multiple nuanced perspectives on instantiated loci of power, Wolf is set adrift. Klein can appreciate that. For how are we meant to keep our heads on straight? How are we meant to map our sense of identity onto a global frame that only gets more complicated by the day? Ultimately, Klein assumes Wolf as her doppelganger to reflect that uncertainty. Maybe, Klein seems to say, that in a slightly different world, she’d have been the one who ventured too far down the rabbit hole, and I’d be reading Wolf’s book instead. Given just how difficult it can be to keep afloat in a post-truth society, maybe Wolf would be the one to make it out and not her.

References Klein, Naomi. Doppelganger: A Trip Into the Mirror World. United States, Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2023. Said, Edward W.. Orientalism (Penguin Modern Classics). United Kingdom, Penguin, 2003.
