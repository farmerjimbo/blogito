hometown

February 9, 2026 · Annie Sheneman

by Annie Sheneman
//

The best advice I got as a twenty-two-year-old came from my friend Esme’s college professor, some guy I’ve never met who teaches theatre at a college I wanted to go to but which did not admit me. The source is less relevant than the advice itself, which was this: you should live at home until it’s holding you back, however long that takes. Esme had kindly offered me this piece of advice in response to my moaning about still living at home with my mother.

I presented this idea to my other coworkers as I stood on a railing, 30 feet from the ground, arms fully extended, to tie a sound cable to a metal pipe. From a nearby ladder, one of them scoffed.

“What, you disagree?” I asked, twisting a nylon cable tie into a bow.

“No,” she said, “I’m just thinking my limit for staying at home is about a week.”

“Hmm,” I said, shuffling to the nearest post to maneuver myself safely down.

I lived in my mother’s house for another year after that conversation. I still live in my small Idaho hometown. As of my next birthday, I’ll have lived here for five years as an adult, not to mention all the years before that.


//


I used to spend my time in high school chemistry class plotting my escape. Not from the class—I liked chemistry—but from the town. From the region. I was, I was certain, going to move east, to New York, Boston, D.C., Baltimore. Maybe even London or Paris. I was going to work in theatre, or, failing that, forensic science. The details mattered less than the distance.

I would, in my mind, be brilliant and valued by coworkers and friends; my hair would be long and glossy; I would be thinner and more athletic than I’d ever actually been; I would carry a leather satchel and ride on public transit. And I would do all this, I was certain, by the age of twenty-five.


//


The only colleges I bothered to look at were on Eastern Time, but I only made it as far away as Ohio. The college Esme went to, in Connecticut, didn’t let me in. Good enough, I figured; at least Ohio was somewhere else, somewhere where fifty minutes of driving got you to a mid-sized city, and not just somewhere smaller than where you’d started out.

Against my will, I was homesick, and I seemed to have some knee-jerk desire to compare everything I saw to where I’d come from. The main street of my college town paled in comparison to the main street of my hometown. The fields didn’t roll off into the distance the way they ought to, and instead of the bluish haze of mountains in the distance, the road sloped up and then was gone.

I irritated my new friends with my constant comparisons, my incessant lists of what we had (good coffee, mountains, walkable cities) or did not have (Panera Bread, the BMV, pierogies) in Idaho.

I was horribly desperate to go home, and once there, everything moved me to tears. The walk to the coffee shop. Renovations to the local library. People who remembered me from childhood. The mural on the exterior of my elementary school. Snow falling gently through the streetlight in front of my mom’s house.

Every time I went back to school, I despaired. Why would I ever leave a place like this?


//


All this to say that I grew up in a place that loved me, and I have never recovered.


//


I graduated college without plans. I moved home. I’ve stayed.

I’m in graduate school now with a bunch of people from all across the country. My friends who’ve moved here for school eventually make some kind of comment to the effect of: this really is a special place. It’s vindicating, I guess. But something at the back of my head hasn’t let go of that chemistry class construction of success, even though it is so transparently the invention of a bored kid who watches too much TV. Something in the back of my mind keeps getting embarrassed about staying.

Where are you from? someone will ask me, and I always just point at the ground. Right here, I say, and something about it is a little excruciating.


//


I’m trying to take Esme’s professor’s advice. I’m just not certain if I can tell the difference between what I want and what I think I ought to want. I haven’t been able to shake my invented idea of who I thought I’d be by now. I also can’t seem to handle the thought of leaving a place that already gives me what I’d be looking for somewhere else. \\
