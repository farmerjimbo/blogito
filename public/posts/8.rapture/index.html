rapture

February 9, 2026 · Jasmine Jones

by Jasmine Jones
//

There is an old woman on the train wearing a bikini top and soiled sweatpants and she is crying into her palms, each cry increasing in volume, until they’re all nearly at a scream. Everyone is pretending not to hear. They are in suits and heels and staring at their phones. Or they are like me, talking on the phone, acting oblivious to avoid the shame. “What’s that noise?” my mother asks.

“Nothing,” I say, staring at my sneakers.

My mother and I talk on the phone every morning while I ride the train to work. The signal will cut out at times and then we will say “hello? hi? you there?” and “no, can’t hear you, wait, yes, hello?” I know this will happen on every single call, but I call her anyway. I prefer to start my morning by worrying for my mother. It gives me something to think about when I’m entering addresses into an endless spreadsheet and staring out my office window at the pretzel stand man. I count how many times he makes new pretzels and replaces the old ones. It is rare. Maybe six times a day.

“Well, anyway,” my mother continues. “I was saying how yesterday I took a nap after lunch and when I woke up, I couldn’t find your dad. I looked everywhere. I went down to the garage and saw his bike was still there, so he couldn’t have gone on a bike ride. His car was there, too. I looked everywhere. He never told me he’d be going anywhere and so I got real panicked. I really thought it happened for a second.” “What happened?”

“Your father was raptured and I was left behind.”

“Oh,” I say.

“But then your father got home from his walk and we laughed about it. He thought I was raptured last week when I was visiting the neighbors.”

She says something else, but I don’t catch it. We’ve gone into a tunnel. The woman walks up and down the train car now, her face streaked in tears, hands shaking and voice strained. She whispers for help now. The only person who makes eye contact with the woman is a toddler. The rest of us are participants in a quiet and orchestrated evil. “Do you feel ready?” my mother’s voice breaks through now.

“For what?”

“The Lord’s return.”

The train stops and the force moves the crying woman forward a few steps, along with the people who have stood up to prepare for their exits. It is as if they’ve all been jolted forward in time and those of us sitting have remained fixed in whatever it is we’ve established on this train car. When those doors open, and they will, they always do, new passengers will pour in and the old will pour out and I will not know what happens in this place after I’ve left it.

The man next to me rises now, one hand on the metal pole, the other clutching his phone. I wait until the doors slide open, and then I stand up. I exit with a small group of people who I watch only by the shoulders so as not to bump into them and violate their personal space.

“I’m at my stop, Mom,” I say. “I’ll call tomorrow.”

We exchange our love you’s and I almost turn around to look for the crying woman, to check if she’s gotten off the train or remained, but the stairs are in front of me now and I’m thinking about work and avoiding the blobs of chewed gum beneath my feet and the light from the city is closer now. It’s breaking over the man who walks in front of me. This bright blinding curtain drifts over him for a moment, disappears his form, and then he returns and I follow him without meaning to. \\
