crickets

February 9, 2026 · Lena Castro

by Lena Castro
//

This is the first time I have seen my father since he got hearing aids. I am home for the holidays, back in my parents’ quiet neighborhood. A respite from the gaudy noise and color of New Orleans, and for once, as we eat dinner around the kitchen table, all three of us converse at a reasonable volume. Hearing aids, miracles, quiet, I thank god.

He held out for over a decade, resistant to such a blatant sign of aging. He’s younger than my mother, but more sensitive about age because his health has never been good. So when we started to notice that he asked constantly for things to be repeated and missed out on large chunks of conversations that took place right in front of him––when he boomed, “Say that again?” across the café table, startling everyone around us and embarrassing both my mother and I, repeatedly, too many times for us not to mention it––he didn’t take our concerns with his normal graceful acquiescence.

It wasn’t his hearing, he said, it was the breakdown of the social order that caused everyone to mumble, and to cover their laughter with their hands, and to look anywhere but in each other’s eyes, and maybe we had conformed but he never would.

I can take the slander to spare your ego, but my mother, a conformist? My mother, a mumbler? Have you not heard her voice cut through the evening newscast, so sharp and clear that everyone watching David Muir must hear her speech coming out of his mouth? Surely if she spoke quietly, her words would not appear as headlines rolling across the chyron. Surely if she didn’t enunciate, the world would not be so in focus for those around her.

My mother began crying all the time. You Never Listen To Me, she said, again and again, headlines rolling, rolling, rolling, and This Isn’t Working, and I Need More From You, and I’m Not Afraid To Live Alone, You Know.

Finally he relented. It is a blessing for us but a mixed bag for him. Conversational strain is reduced. It is only once I speak to him with my back turned, and he responds normally, that I realize how much he used to rely on lip reading. We take a walk through piles of autumn leaves, and he crunches happily through them like a child. “They sound so crisp!” he exclaims joyfully. The guitar is not so gentle as before. It’s tinny now, more percussive, the Rolling Stones when he wants Donovan. He takes the hearing aids out when he plays, but then he can’t hear my mother shout a compliment from the kitchen.

We finish dinner, clear the dishes, they sit back at the table for a game of Scrabble.

“Do you have your hearing aids in?”

“Yes.”

“Is it loud? The clacking of the tiles, is it too loud?”

“No, it’s not too loud.”

“Can you hear the crickets?”

“No.”

“Oh. I thought you’d be able to hear them now.”

My mother plays TERRORS. Bingo, double letter score, 65 points. My father plays HUM. He concatenates it with the word AN to make HUMAN. Triple word score. 30 points.

How many years has it been, Papa, since you heard the crickets? Sometimes loving annoyance comes into Mother’s voice at high tones, but is the frequency just out of your reach? Have you been missing it all this time? In your watery, dulled, cricketless world, what does evening sound like? \\
