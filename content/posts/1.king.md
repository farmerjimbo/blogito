by Kasey Kirchner  
//  
<br>

The king, being a king, got everything he wanted—every dream fulfilled, every hunger sated, every fleeting whim or fancy realized. If he wanted to bathe in rose petals, his devoted servants would brave the thorns. If he wanted to bathe in honey, they would brave the bees. If he wanted to bathe in ox blood, they would brave the beast.  

Bathing was the king’s favorite pastime. His extravagant golden tub was large enough to accommodate three people, and he often invited guests to partake in these pleasures with him. He had his favorites, of course, but after a while, he grew bored of their familiar movements, their predictable sounds. It was not uncommon for his excitement to evaporate and for his mood to plummet drastically and without warning. When this happened, he would banish his guests from the bathing room or push himself out of the bath and wander back to bed, dripping and sliding naked through the halls.  

To fend off his boredom, he began to demand new things. He no longer wanted to bathe in mere water, but in everything else the kingdom could give him. He wanted to sense every texture, every temperature. To be touched, held, by everything.  

 He quickly exhausted all the liquids, bathing in wine, then ale, then milk, then tea. Then came the foods—berries, flour, oil, butter. Then came the more experimental ingredients: loamy soil; alpaca wool; butterfly wings. He was almost out of ideas when, during a bath in gold coins, he saw that one of his guests was weeping. She had turned away from him so he wouldn’t see, but he caught her chin and pulled it toward him.  

“Tears,” he said. “Human tears.”  

For his next bath, he ordered his servants to fill the tub with tears. They looked at one another, then back at him. “Tears, Your Majesty?”  

In response, he smacked the youngest boy hard across the face. Then he waited a moment until the boy’s eyes welled. “Those,” he said, pointing at the tears now running down the boy’s face. “I want those.”  

The king turned on his heel and left, and as he did so, the other servants hurried to collect the boy’s tears, holding earthen mugs beneath each of his eyes.   

“Keep crying,” they told him. And he did.   

Of course, one boy’s tears weren’t nearly enough to fill the king’s enormous tub, so the servants spread out among the castle’s back rooms, locating everyone in their caste. For most, all they had to do was ask. “The king wants your tears. Quick, think of something sad.” For others, they had to get creative. They held onions up to each other’s faces and threw dust in each other’s eyes. For the more stubborn servants, they resorted to pinching, pulling hair, and burning. They stayed up all through the night, ordering a stable boy to recount the tale of his lost love to all the servant women, then hurried around the weeping audience to collect their tears. For the children, they employed the court jester, who made them laugh until they cried. For the older servants, those with gray hair and hardened hearts, they played music.  

By the morning, they had collected tears from every servant, but it wasn’t enough. Barely enough to cover the base of the tub, in fact. With the king on his way to the bathing room, they had to act quickly. They filled the rest of the tub with rainwater, praying he wouldn’t notice.  

When at last the king shed his robe and lowered himself into the golden basin, the servants held their breath.  

He drew his hands across the surface of the water, then lifted his fingers and watched the droplets fall. “Are my people so unhappy?” he whispered. Then he snapped his head in the direction of the servants, slapped the surface of the water, and bellowed, “Are my people so unhappy that they can fill this tub with tears in a single night?”  

The servants, eyes red and puffy, glanced at each other.  

“Answer me!”   

“No, Your Majesty,” said the youngest servant. “Some of them are happy tears.”  

The king glared. “Happy tears?”  

“Yes, Your Majesty.”  

“You lie!” he wailed, splashing so violently that much of the water sloshed over the sides of the tub. “There is no such thing as happy tears. I am the happiest man in the entire kingdom and I have never shed such a tear.”  

His splashing stopped then, and his arms grew still. He sat there, face blotchy with something like rage, as the water settled around him. The servants, stationed by the door, said nothing. 
Finally, the king pushed himself out of the tub, unplugged the drain, and let the liquid seep over the floor. He strolled toward the door of the bathing room, wrapping his robe around him, and leaned toward the servant who had spoken. There was a tear caught in the corner of his eye. But was it a tear of his own or a tear from the bath—the servant couldn’t tell.  

“Tomorrow, I shall bathe in birch sap,” he said. Then he left, and the servants got to work. \\\\
