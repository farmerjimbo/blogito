by Ethan Miller  
//  
<br>

There are those who believe they can manipulate fate. They make promises, offer systems. To manifest the life you want you must focus your mind on the scene you will inhabit once your wildest dreams have been realized. They’ll tell you the quality of your life depends on your resolve to realise your will upon the world.  

These teachers position themselves opposite the Original Religions whose profits and priests bemoan this kind of brazen self-assuredness. The old way dictates that one should pray or meditate in a disinterested way. The point is to commune with the divine, not to realise one’s egoic interests. Fate, they tell us, is out of our hands.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br>

Recently my friend and roommate of three years took his own life. Within about an hour of hearing the news I broke all my rules: I bought a pack of cigarettes and called my ex-girlfriend. We stayed up all night talking about everything but the news. The only way we could get at it was silence.  

About a year into dating her, I decided I would become healthy. I thought I was doing it for her, but looking back that’s when we started drifting apart. First I quit smoking; then I started doing yoga; and then, at the behest of my teachers, I began interrogating attachment. About six months after finishing undergrad, I came to view our relationship as just another soft cushion I kept between me and the harsh truth of reality.  

I had wondered for a long time what kind of spiritual distress might loosen my convictions about health. That night I found out.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br> 

Sometimes in college, beset by insomnia, I would head to the porch of our rickety New England house to have a cigarette around two or three in the morning. Life as an undergraduate had a pleasant sense of being only a dress rehearsal and so I felt a minimum of guilt about the habit. Often, I would find my roommate there—cigarette in hand, music playing from his phone—seeming as if he’d been out there all night.  

Sometimes I would try to get at what was bothering me, the reason I couldn’t sleep, but I was always struck by how inadequate the words seemed to describe what I was experiencing. My roommate would make his own attempts to give shape to his suffering, but these would usually fizzle out, lost in the ambiguity of smoke and fatigue. Later, we decided it was best not to focus on our discontents.  

We were brothers in the blues, me and my roommate, so when I started getting better, when I learned how not to dwell in the low place, I felt like I was leaving him behind. When I told him what I’d done to get better, I could tell he was listening hard, but the same strategies never seemed to work for him.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br> 

In the wake of my breakup, I ramped up my once casual interest in Buddhism to the level of devotion. Buddhism teaches its practitioners to reconcile themselves with reality. To meditate is to learn to love what is.  

As the adult accompaniments of the life I shared with my ex fell away, I crash-landed in my childhood bedroom. Great, I thought, it’s like a test. I went about trying to love a situation that was preposterously at odds with my concept of myself.  

Somewhere in the back of my mind, I had it fixed that once I found contentment, everything would change: a new love would spur new adventures or the perfect vocation would be irresistibly attracted to my serene state of self-possession. I thought it was like a video game level you could pass by simply relinquishing the desire to pass in the first place.  

As the months ticked by and no one had descended from on high to deliver me from my mundanity, my dedication to Buddhism waned. I couldn’t stay devoted to a system of belief that asked me to surrender my desires when I so desperately wanted my life to change.  

Eventually, I turned to the manifestation teachers filling YouTube with starry-eyed promises of total control. If I couldn’t learn to accept my life, maybe they could help me shape it.  

Upon their instruction, I thought of every good thing that had ever come into my life—professional opportunities, loving relationships, wild adventures—and tried to reverse engineer the psychic conditions that had produced them. I couldn’t remember feeling particularly worthy of these windfalls. From what I could tell, self-worth had followed from my ability to rise to the challenges life presented me, but the teachers told me that self-belief was the crux on which my dream life depended.  

I followed their advice, ignored the reality of my surroundings, and did my best to just believe.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br> 

I never would’ve started smoking in the first place if my brother was still alive. In high school, I had tried to live up to his example as a successful long-distance runner. He died six months before I left home for college, and in the traumatic debris of family disarray, I found what I had long been unknowingly looking for: permission.  

Permission to do whatever I wanted. Permission not to care what our mom and dad thought. Permission to live as if we were all going to die, because now I had proof that we were.  

It was during this time of abandon that most of the exciting, improbable things that ever happened to me took place. It was when I met my roommate through a lengthy chain of friends, started playing shows with musicians well out of my league, and even got a job for the unemployable skill of being able to read Old French.  

In fact, the loving face of my ex appeared not out of the angelic glow of an earned self-worth, but instead out of the drug-addled haze of a life lived without regard for its own continuation.  

I try not to romanticize an attitude that put so much strain on so many dear relationships, but I can’t ignore the proof: cigarettes, while horrible for my lungs, do wonders for my nervous and tense disposition.  

When I smoke, I remember the meaning of pleasure.   
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br>

On the phone, my ex told me she’d seen my roommate in New York a few months ago. He’d been with the same girl he was with during college, an impossibly good match for him and his strange array of interests. She told me he’d seemed happy.  

Me and my roommate had played phone tag for the last year and it was then that I realized the game had ended. The last time we’d talked, I made it seem like everything was working out for me. At the time, I probably felt like it was. I wish I had called him when I didn’t.  

After my ex went to bed, I sat up thinking about how nice it might be to invite her out to the city I just moved to, how strange it was that we still got along so well. We could have a good time together, like we did for a night back in the winter, and we wouldn’t have to think about the incompatibilities we both acknowledged at the end.  

It could just be nice.  

I typed out the invitation, but I never sent it.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br> 

One technique manifestation teachers employ is to identify what they call your core wound, the hurt at the bottom of all the ways you don’t believe in yourself. After about 20 minutes of introspection, I found mine: I do not, despite evidence to the contrary, feel loved.  

Then, they tell you, come up with a mantra that counteracts this wound. Convince yourself you don’t have it. Mine was easy: I am loved.  

I repeated this affirmation to myself with wild conviction this past summer. I managed to run it as a constant loop, so that even when I was ordering a sandwich or talking to a friend, somewhere in the back of my mind I could hear it: I am loved.  

The thinking with this kind of practice is that by embodying this positive state, you will in turn attract that which you need to feel it, the proof.  

I didn’t receive love, exactly, but rather an instance of companionship. On a recent flight, while looping this mantra, a persistent old man managed to wrangle me into conversation. I had initially decided I hated him because in the sweaty heat of the tarmac-grounded plane, he asked me to please move my knee into my own personal space. Later, though, he told me he was going to see a woman he had loved during the war, now that his wife had been dead for over a decade.  

I just sent her an email, he said, I had no idea if she’d respond. Turns out her husband had died too!  

He didn’t couch this hope in anything other than the simple conviction that it might work out. It would be months before his lost love responded to his initial email.  

When we parted, he asked me to take a selfie on his phone in case his granddaughter might be interested in me. She’s eligible, he said, and pretty, he assured me when he saw my reluctant look.  

I took the selfie and gave him my number, just in case.  
<br>
<center>//</center>  
<br><br>

As the weight of my friend’s death set in, the words of the YouTube gurus began to seem brittle and thin. After a while, what once seemed like wisdom started to seem like advertising, and I found my finger hovering above links to paywalls behind which the real secrets were said to lie. Real wisdom, frustratingly, tends to find us and rarely arrives while prostrate, flicking through images on a screen.  

On a walk through my new city, I remembered a time years ago, during COVID, when I worked at an apple orchard in southwest Missouri. My first cigarette habit was in full swing, and I was stunned every time I walked in the door with ash-covered fingertips and my parents didn’t notice.  

Every morning I’d pull myself out of a weed-fueled haze, put on my muck boots, and traipse out to the trees to pick apples and listen to the owner, Mike, tell me about his life. 
He was a musician so I told him that me and my friends were organizing a secret outdoor show. Mike was old but still astute. He noticed the way I carried tension in my body: white knuckling stems which could’ve been loosed by almost no effort at all.  

On the morning of the show, I told him I was excited, but he could see I was really nervous. He prodded at me, and I assured him we were well rehearsed, that all the details had been accounted for.  

He didn’t think this was how a musician ought to approach his work.  

Just play it loose, he said.  

I did my best to listen. \\\\
