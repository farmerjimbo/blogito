A sharp clamor ricocheted through the morning quiet. Gulls sprang from their roosts above drystone homes, squawking in protest. One squabble of birds drifted over to investigate the commotion and spied a human passing through the dying Koumaria shrubs at the northern edge of the village. The creature labored through rocky soil. As it walked, a narrow path through the shrubs grew in its wake. From afar, the gulls studied the figure and the object it dragged to carve the path. The item was shiny and noisy and altogether inedible. This upset the gulls. They made their opinion known by defecating globs of pasty waste onto the figure before returning to their roosts in the village.

Down in the village, the Goodwoman Sonja knelt before an apricot sapling, inspecting the plant for blight. She wiped sweat from her eyes to get a clear look. One of the plant’s roots dangled limply but bore no mark of rot. Content, she prepared to set the sapling in the earth but was interrupted by the noise that had so bothered the gulls. She couldn’t place the peculiar sound. She left the promising tree, dusted off her dress, and poked her head around the inlet of her garden wall. Other women, stooped women with sun-crinkled faces, quit their work to investigate the noise as well. Their husbands and fathers and sons were still at sea. They had left before dawn for the morning catch. Sonja met the gaze of her neighbor across the path, and the two shared a moment, each raising a brow in concern before returning their attention to the source of their mutual confusion.

Up the cobbles, too distant to make clear, she spotted a figure. Sonja’s vision had been failing her for some years, and even as she strained her eyes, she could not recognize the silhouette above her. She could, however, see what he dragged with him.

The anchor was massive, larger than any she had ever seen in a lifetime among fishermen. The man—or adolescent, she now realized—gripped the broad stock in front of him and walked backwards over the stones, his bare feet tripping on protruding rocks and weeds. When he reached a gap in the path, he lost control of the anchor momentarily as it flung into the air, fighting him for control. 

As the figure, the anchor, and the gulls drew closer, Sonja covered her ears to drown out the noise at her doorstep. Only then—fingers plunged into her ears—did she recognize him. It was the orchard boy from the farm north of the village. The realization wearied her. Nothing good came from that farm, she thought. The family lived in the old ways. Kept beliefs long since abandoned by the village—heretical beliefs that Sonja had never felt comfortable living near. When her own son had been a young child, he’d played with the girl from the farm, and Sonja had befriended the family’s mother. However agreeable the woman had been, Sonja remained wary of the family. Harsh gods made for harsh people.

She lowered her hands to form a tight grasp before her skirt. The sound of clanging metal returned. Her gaze followed her hands down, and without thinking, she began to rub the creases of her right hand with the thumb of her left, back and forth, feeling the deep crevasses of her worn palm. The sun burned hot overhead. 

She brought her gaze up, only to see that the boy and his lonely parade had passed. Out again, she peered around the edge of the garden. The boy labored directly before her. Sweat stuck to his thick black curls from his forehead to his nape. 

“Boy, what is that thing?”

If he heard her over the noise, he did not acknowledge it. His focus held fast to the anchor he wrestled between his strained arms. 

“Goodness\! 

BOY\!

Stop, please\!”

She was sure that her voice, now full in its character, had reached him, yet the boy did nothing but pause for a single moment. He then turned around with some difficulty and loaded the anchor’s weight above his shoulder blades. The head of the metal shank protruded past his own. Its awkwardness meant he could look only down as he lumbered forward.

The rock formation, known locally as the Salt Mistress, jutted from the beach just east of the village’s dock. Like the figurehead on a massive ship, the Mistress grew out from the low cliffs on her journey to the sea. Shallow puddles dotted the beach on either side of the promontory. Over the course of a year, the puddles would fall prey to the sun as it stole away their water, leaving only salty residue. For now, though, the puddles sat half full.

Old-man Palinkos sat on top of the Salt Mistress, a fishing rod in his hands, each missing a half-finger or two. He had stopped going out on the boats after the last accident. Now each morning, he carried his self-fashioned rod and stool to the rocks and cast uncaringly into the lapping waves below. He often thought about the sea or the gulls or the wind; today, he thought about the salt holes. *They are ugly little pocks*, he thought. The beach flowed so naturally as it crumbled from the cliffs above and out into the sea, and the Mistress, with her grace and jagged power, gave the cove character. *The holes spoil her*. He would never admit it to his wife, but the Mistress was his second love. 

A screech brought him out of his day’s ruminations. He looked down the rocks and over the ponds, up past the dock and toward the village. There, he saw children playing. Some of them danced or cartwheeled while others ducked between shaded alcoves in frightful curiosity. Underfoot, dogs yipped and yapped in every direction, uncertain of where to point their ire. Overhead, gulls and village women observed the scene. 

The young man at the center of the chaos seemed oblivious to the whole affair. With his back to Palinkos, dragging an anchor nearly twice his size, the figure heaved in short, powerful bursts as he moved closer to the top of the stairs that separated the village from the jutty. After pausing for a half-dozen heartbeats, the boy began the climb down the stairs. There were one hundred and twenty-seven steps from the last precipice of the village to the foot of the docks. This, Palinkos knew, not from ever counting but just as a matter of fact. He also knew that the stairs had been carved from the cliffs long ago. Half were taller than his knees, others barely steps at all.

“You descend the steps of giants and mice\! Which are you?” Palinkos shouted at the boy, knowing full well that the gibe would not reach his ears. Still, he felt pleased with his wit.

His glee faded as he, for the first time, saw the commotion for what it was. The crowd, led by children, suggested a celebration, but the boy’s face, twisted in anguish, betrayed the moment’s brutality. Dried blood covered his arms. The anchor could not be dragged down the larger steps, so, Palinkos saw, the poor creature improvised a complicated motion. The boy positioned himself with one foot on two successive steps and drew himself close. Summoning strength, he then used locked arms and a tense swivel to lift the hulking metal briefly into the air and down onto the step below. A breath, one, two. Then again, he repeated the process, skipping altogether the steps intended for Palinkos’ mice.

Atop the Mistress, the aged fisherman—now standing—watched in shock. Each step sickened him. The anchor dwarfed the man-child, and each crashing drop exposed its impossible weight. 

Up above, the village children no longer danced or shouted; all stood—together with the mothers and sisters—in silence. Below, the boy cleared the stairs and now dragged the anchor across the final path before the docks. As he crossed onto the wooden planks of the docks, the anchor ceased to shriek and began thudding against each board. The relative quiet gave Palinkos his first chance to communicate across the water.

“My boy, what are you doing?” he shouted, standing up from his stool.

“Why bring this instrument to the sea? It may have the look of a seafaring tool, but that anchor is much too large to do you any good.”

He knew he had been heard, but his question was met with only further thudding monotony. 

“You shouldn’t believe in her. Those gods are gone.”

At this, the boy finally stopped his relentless march. He made not the slightest turn of head nor twitch of arm, keeping the anchor motionless in front of him. Sweat poured down from his forehead in viscous drops and drenched his simple shirt along its length.

Palinkos seized the moment: “What, dear Soilson, do you hope to achieve? The farm is gone—your father too. A watery grave will help no one.”

A moment passed.

“A gift,” the boy said, turning to Palinkos. His voice shook: “I must give a gift.” With his head again directed down at his grip, he tensed his forearms and continued down the dock. 

Palinkos sank back onto his stool. At the end of the dock, the boy rolled the anchor onto a small dinghy and set out to sea.

Yori kept a strong, even pace over the water. On any other day the rowing would have been grueling, but today, following his morning labors, he welcomed the motion. His earlier theatrics stuck as embarrassment to his ribs. He regretted the heed he had not paid the old woman, a friend of his mother’s. Instead of dwelling, though, he forced himself to focus on the task at hand. 

A great expanse of open water lifted behind his back—and increasingly before him—and he wondered whether the dinghy would survive the journey. The boat had been his uncle’s and even in its younger years had never been much to look at. Now in the light of day, he could see the woodworm holes eating at the hull. The boat floated on its last legs. Yori made certain not to pull the oars toward the sky while he rowed. He feared he might dislocate the oarlocks that tenuously held to the gunwale. 

A warm breeze channeled through his shirt. He rowed some time longer, lost in unthought, first southeast and then south, until the sun blazed directly overhead. Away from the reefs and sand banks, the sea swelled before him. 

With methodical hands, he drew out a thin rope stored beneath his feet and secured one end around the eye of the anchor. Dotingly, he laid the anchor flat against the thwarts so that the metal weight lay parallel to the sea. Yori then turned around and braced himself for the discomfort ahead. Facing the sky, he draped his body over the anchor and planted his feet on the deck. The pain as he lowered himself down was clear and frictional, like kneeling on a stone floor during prayer. Now supine, he took the remaining rope and began wrapping it around his chest and then under the anchor in tight, even circles. The work was slow. Passing the rope from hand to hand under the increasingly taut binding sapped his energy. He felt satisfied once he lashed himself from chest to upper thigh. The bar felt cool against his back. The numbing spread down his limbs and up through his spine, and for a fraction of a moment, he experienced a cool paradise.

Yori came back to his body. He inhaled a jet of air, then he pushed off hard with his right foot against the boards, attempting to roll him and the anchor over the gunwale. Despite his effort, the anchor barely lifted. Again, he drew up strength and shoved his might down to kick off, yet the cord around him kept him from using his trunk for power. 

Yori lay there for twenty heartbeats, panting like a pregnant dog. The day’s physical stresses had caught up with him. 

He hooked his fingers through the oarlocks with arms outstretched and began rocking the dinghy from side to side. The motion required him to put his weight on one side of the anchor before quickly shifting to the other, forcing his vertebrae to grind back and forth over the rod. Momentum grew, and soon he could see the water again by craning his neck. He, the boat, the oars, and the anchor tossed their collective weight in frenzied sweeps. One of the oarlocks ripped from the wooden gunwale, so he clung to the edge as best he could to continue the swing. Swirling tides and gusts pitched the boat sideways. Just as the dinghy would reach its apex—just as he could picture crashing into the water below—it would roll over once more. Metal scraped bone. The oars slid out. Back again to the left, until he and the anchor hung to gravity’s precipice. He stared at the blue directly below to his left, then the boat took one last furious rock and threw the two companions into the depths.

The sound of thwomping woke Yori. His heavy eyes curled open, then shut again in protest. The sun careened through the branches above and onto his hiding spot among the roots. Sweat dried on his chest. The taste of overripe date stickied his mouth. Lifting his head, Yori could see axes swinging against a tree. A man broke away from the group of fellers to approach him.

“Come, Yori. We let you rest. Now we must begin again.”

The boy made no motion to rise. “Stefan, we cannot do this. There must be another way.”

“We’ve discussed this. And Ophelia agreed. They all must go. We cannot tempt fate, pox or no pox.”

The man’s cowardice had always bothered Yori. And since joining the village priestdom, Stefan’s righteousness had only grown more insufferable. His service to his young gods was all devotion and no faith. Empty zealotry, Yori thought. 

“There’s still a chance it hasn’t spread. Let’s burn the blighted roots. It can be done, as my father said. With care, we could try again.”

“Truly, Yori? Have you learned nothing?” Stefan’s eyes narrowed. He twirled the base of his axe into the soil. “The Open Palm have made their will known. The poison spreads, and the wood must go.”

“Don’t talk to me about your phony gods. Have they answered any of your prayers yet, Stefan? Are they listening now?” Yori heard the venom in his voice. “We won’t have a harvest again for a century. You know this.”

“Ophelia and I, we have decided. Even if you don’t trust me, trust her.”

Stefan’s leathered hand floated before him. Yori pushed himself to his feet instead.

“What good was our last gift if now we destroy my father’s work? Can you explain that to me, Stefan?” 

“Only the gods know.” Stefan pondered. “Now let’s be done with this.”

Yori scowled as he strode past Stefan, heading in the direction of the work team. With Stefan trailing, they crossed through the field toward the olive orchard. On the ground lay the carcasses of ancient trees. Their twisted bodies bled black and yellow pus. 

He picked up an axe, and they worked at destroying his father’s orchard. One tree to the next.

Light curled at odd trajectories under the water’s surface. Rays hit the surface and wound around one another before shooting off unpredictably. Tiny microbes fell off the tangled light and clustered together as drifting stars. 

Yori reached out to touch one of the descending starfalls and found it surprisingly heavy. Not cumbersome, but similar in heft to a skipping stone. With the starfall in hand, he felt a shock of overwhelming hunger. Its weight and size and smoothness reminded him of a fig fruit, plucked by the hands of his sister and tossed down from the branches above. 

Starving, he plopped the starfall into his mouth.

The gift was salty, brined in the juices of the sea. Delightfully chewy, moreish. His father would love them, he decided, for he cherished all things salty. His mother would ponder how to make one. ‘Equal parts salt and light,’ she would have guessed, ‘But that suppleness will be difficult to recreate.’ He shoveled more of the treats into his mouth.

As they sank alongside the boy and anchor, the starfalls began crashing into one another. Upon impact, they fused into larger globules: first as figs, then oranges, then ballooning melons. The light fought to break free of these growing spheres. Spurts of raw energy flared out like fingers stretching through rolled dough. In his stomach, Yori could feel the weight of the starfalls he’d eaten. They burgeoned for space within him. His innards, though bound by rope, began to expand. He looked around, sinking ever further below the surface as the array of miniature suns quivered ever more violently, both within and without. In his gut, the energy fought to escape. 

Suddenly, one of the massive balls before him exploded in a flash of harsh light. The sudden release sent a shockwave through the water. A second shot off, then a third, until the entire sea ignited in a brilliant display. His stomach flipped, and out of his mouth, cascading from recesses wormed within, poured a flood of dense starmatter. 

The sudden purge left him strangely sated. Some residue of the starmatter remained within.

When the light of the explosions began to fade—eaten by the sea—he could no longer see the surface above. Color sapped from the water. Goose-pimples speckled his arms. The infinite sea closed in around them as they drifted down.

The rain had squelched the olivewood bonfires. Ophelia watched the downpour from under a canopy where once they had pressed the olives into oil. Their work—days of manic labor cutting down the orchard, the product of a half-dozen generations’ worth of toil—had been rejected. Now the sickly trees wasted away in the mud. *No sacrifice will reach these rancid gods*, she thought.

Ophelia hacked at a sodden olive branch with a carving knife while her husband wiped grime from his eyes. Out of the corner of her eye, she watched Yori as he stared blankly at the wasteland before them, his arms and face covered in resinous slime. Stefan knelt near her in a heap of discarded harvesting nets. The man muttered an inaudible chant under his breath.

“Prayers won’t help us, Stefan,” Ophelia let out, more out of pity than ire. Her husband’s religiosity usually irritated her, but on such a woeful day, she couldn’t bring herself to be angry with him. He had labored, as they all had, through the nightmare of ash and splinter. Now, she only wished to save her husband from his hope.

Stefan managed to lift his gaze to meet hers. His cheeks were smeared in a black oil where he had dragged his fingers across his eyes. The effect wilded her husband. It reminded her of the mischief-maker she had married. 

“We’ll try again when the wood dries. It may still work,” he affirmed. 

Ophelia considered him. “No, Stef, it will not. And I’m tired. I refuse to continue groveling to spirits that curse us.” At this, Stefan sank deeper into the mesh beneath him. “Regardless, we are too late,” she continued. “It’s already spread. The village will show signs soon, if not already. And when it does, those fisherwives and drunks will panic.”

Stefan recoiled at her sour words. “What then? You would just let them suffer?”

Ophelia foresaw the destination of their brewing argument. The foundations of it had been laid through a dozen years’ worth of skirmishes. 

“Enough, Stef. You know what I am going to say.” Her lips curled to the side as she bit the inside of her lip. When Stefan said nothing, she spoke again, “We must go see Mother Keeper. We should have gone from the beginning.”

At this, she saw Yori’s head lift. Stefan reacted more forcefully. He awkwardly unspooled himself from the netting and shot up to meet her directly. He towered over her. For this, she didn’t blame him. His habit of unconscious physical intimidation had never come to anything. His use of his stature was a last-ditch effort: This plea required his full body. 

 “You cannot. I won’t let that… cult dictate the future of my… our family.” Stefan’s voice grew pained. “Your father was a brave man, certainly, yes, but he was also a fool. I knew it when he left. He was foolish to put his faith in such evil. That *temptress*… whatever she is, she is no god. You swore against her and that entire heresy. When we married, we did so under the grace of the Open Palm. You agreed to that\!” 

Ophelia felt the warm air that billowed out of her husband’s nostrils. 

“What would you even give her? We have nothing. Nothing\!” he shouted.

Their faces nearly touched during the height of his animation. Now his exhales slowed. She still held the knife and hunk of olivewood, which he now seemed to remember. He stepped back with hands raised, head down in apology. The man, so enthralled moments ago, shrunk.

“What more can we lose?” he nearly whispered.

Ophelia turned away from her husband and strode toward Yori. Her brother still pretended to ignore them. She imagined his grim thoughts as he observed their family land before them. The unlit bonfires of the plagued trees captured only their latest loss. The farm had been fading for some time. The once-rich soil had been dredged of its quality by years of neglect. The fig grove, once her favorite part of the property, had long ago been converted into ramshackle housing for laborers. Now, runnels of rainwater split the earth between caked anthill mounds the height of a human. Their inheritance had been dying long before the blight visited them.

She grabbed her brother’s shoulder and spun him around to face her. “Yori, look at me.” And he reluctantly did.

With mud-crusted fingers, she handed him the wooden implement she’d carved from the dead branch: a T-shaped hunk of soot-black wood. As he took the shape from her hands, Yori’s eyes searched for explanation. She answered his unspoken question by wrapping her hand around his. “Yori,” she began quietly in his ear, “You remember how father referred to her, don’t you? Mother Keeper, the Forgotten Matron of the Good Path. She is the only one who can end this madness.”

Yori faltered a half-step back into the mud.

Ophelia let him take the wooden cross as she pressed the moment: “She will provide the path forward. She will make everything *right*.”

Behind them, Stefan sputtered, “What are you threatening him with, Ophelia?”

 “She rewards those who seek her out\!” Ophelia continued, now loud enough for her husband to hear. “Yori, we will meet her. I will guide you, and you will make our appeal.”

The sound of Stefan’s growing pleas buzzed behind them as empty noise. She focused only on her brother as he spun the wood in his hand. 

“How do we know?” Yori asked her. “How do we know what she wants? How do we know she will make things right? She didn’t with father. That didn’t fix things. Why would this time be any different?”

“You will come back,” Ophelia exhaled. “You won’t end up like father.”

They landed headfirst; the sandy sea floor took him by surprise. Slowly, the anchor fell flat without a sound. Yori, in total darkness, lay like a drowned starfish. Icy convulsions—in his shoulders, neck, thighs—wormed beneath his skin. The effect lingered long enough for him to worry it wouldn’t cease. Finally, when the convulsions waned, his shoulders drooped back to rest on the anchor. The metal stock relaxed him, two solid arms onto which he could collapse.

The rope resisted his first attempt to untie it. He strained to lift his arms to the knot around the anchor’s eye. The effort left him dizzy, and he collapsed down. On his second attempt, the knot gave way. To loosen the lashing around his chest, Yori had to crunch his stomach and lift the entire anchor—made possible by their buoyancy—while also pulling the rope through. The motion, which he performed several times, rammed his back into the anchor’s shaft. With three fingers’ width of slack, he managed to shimmy himself out of his self-imposed noose.

His back meat felt raw. The muscles of his right leg cramped badly. 

*I’ll have to drag that as well,* he thought.

He closed his eyes and pressed his fingers into the inflamed tissue along his thigh. For thirty heartbeats he stood, investigating the muscles with curiosity. Then, eyes open, he tied a bowline through the anchor’s eye once more, threw the rope over his shoulder, and started a final, plodding march along the nightfloor. 

Dense sand grabbed at his dragging foot. Fear of sinking into the seabed kept him moving down an increasingly steep dune. The anchor kept pace behind him.

Yori made to speak, failed at that, and reckoned with the great silence around him. In his mind, instead, an entreaty:

*Father, there is darkness here. I’m full of it. Did Ophelia know where she would take us? Or had you taken this secret with you as well?* 

*Forgive me. I didn’t know it would be this difficult.* 

When the slope began to even out, he looked over his head to check the rope, which, somehow, had not frayed. His wandering mind did not immediately register that he could *see* the braided fabric. An anemic glow before him gave shape to the rope and surrounding seabed. His body looked sickly in the vague light: bones protruding through wan skin. 

Yori hobbled over to the anchor and patted its stock. “Almost there. You can rest when we arrive,” he sighed, then headed toward the source of the light. 

A path grew from the naked seabed and narrowed until they reached, at the meeting point of two obsidian curtains, a cavern entrance. Unruly sand evened out to flagstone as he approached. The light, now harsh to his eyes, took on a greenish hue and threw swimming shadows onto the black walls enveloping them. At the entrance, Yori hesitated for only a moment, then limped through the gash at the bottom of the sea.

Down, centered at the base of the cave, he saw a pool, green as unripe quince and perfectly circular at ten lengths across. All around the pool lay a trove of misplaced items: elaborate silver serving trays, a puffy red dress, scimitars and axes and clubs, unrecognizable instruments with dozens of strings, a spherical map the size of the anchor, a metal contraption with wheels and a strange seat, as well as hundreds of other objects he could not recognize. The display transfigured in the shifting light, highlighting different areas of the bizarre collection with each passing moment. 

Yori thought to move forward, closer to the pool, but, with frustration, could not find his balance. The menagerie kept him in place. He sought to ground his vision by focusing on a singular item on the wall. The map sphere drew his eye, yet as he locked his gaze closer to the curiosity, it started to rotate, first slowly, then rapidly, bloating itself along a sidelong axis. The motion distorted his sense of space. The walls of the cavern moved in, folded out, fell as boulders, then crawled toward him. 

Behind her battery of eyes, Acheiropoita, Mother Keeper, watched the human struggle. His skin was marred by lines of discolored abrasions. Fabric and hair clung to his limbs. She could feel the vibrations of his labored breath, as well as the resonance given off by the silver object behind him. That intrigued her. Slowly, she slithered her arms down from her roost above the pool. 

Feeling her descent, he looked up.

The boy’s heartbeat revealed his fear, though he only shrunk back slightly in defense of the object. Sensing the change, Acheiropoita coiled two of her tentacles in spiral mounds before the human in a silent offering of peace.

“I’ve come to give a gift,” the human cried, drowning out his obvious discomfort. “I’ve come to end the blight.”

“You come for absolution,” she retorted. The membranous tissue and teeth she used to speak felt inflexible from disuse. 

*Another stricken child.* 

“Let me see you,” she called. Unwinding from her roost, she descended.

Yori froze. His bravery had run its course; this *thing* was not what he had expected. The body, twenty lengths tall, was of twisted pink tissue and splotchy black whiskers. Mounds of pulpy flesh traced upwards in a vulgar cone. A dozen tentacles splayed from the base of the body, half as warped legs and half as cloying arms, two still coiled before him. Mounted toward the top of the cone, like a ram’s horn on a wall, was a plate of treasures—animal pelts, human limbs, and fossilized arthropods. The plate filled the regions between its hundreds of black, watchful eyes. And at the base of the eyes, appended without care, the head of his father, shuttered and sickly pale, dangled at an off angle. The sight revolted him.

“How do you come to this place?” asked Acheiropoita, the words mangled together, produced by unhuman organs.

“My father,” he stumbled, “journeyed here before. I saw it in his dreams.”

“You can see others’ dreams? Walkers, you serve the sea in unusual patterns. So separate. So not. That is a gift.”

“Will you let me make an offering?” Yori trembled.

“You? *You*, dream-stealer?” The cavern filled with her gnashing language. “You offer nothing\!”

Yori’s mind scrambled for a response. “The anchor… The anchor is yours\! She is our gift to the Forgotten Matron\! A prize for your collection.”

“A prize, surely, but not yours.” 

Yori watched as her eyes fluttered rapidly. Then, grabbing unseen hooks on the cavern ceiling, Mother Keeper swung toward the boy with terrible speed. There was no time for him to react. She wrapped one of her hairy tentacles tightly around him and hoisted him close to its contorted face, near the remains attached to the bizarre plate of trophies. Another tentacle drew up next to him. The god, prying at his head, forcibly opened his jaw and slithered down his esophagus. 

Her search would be quick. She dug through his entrails, beyond his scarred tissue, until she found the residue. There, she lingered, lightly pressing on the starfall. The Salt Mistress had given the boy something wondrous—a prize he could not even begin to appreciate. It pulsated at her touch. 

Although she resented her role in these events, Acheiropoita would pass along the dead goddess’ blessing. 

It was necessary that she intervene. This blight could not continue. She knew that those grotesque godlings were to blame. Unchecked, their influence had spread in the domain above. She had been careless. One of the Open Palm could never hold sway over the people, but together, the godlings posed a threat to her. Their new pact reeked of ambition. Young deities empowered by desperate followers. A dangerous combination, she thought. Yes, she would grant the walker’s request, but not for the reasons he expected.

As the rubbery tissue dragged out from him, his body reflexively tried to purge, but the retching produced only bile in his throat. Spindly arms set him down on the cavern floor where he curled himself into a coughing fit. 

Almost coyly, the god slunk beside the swirling pool. 

Recovering from such violation, Yori could not bring himself to look at the Mother again as he rolled onto hands and knees. 

“Why?” he coughed out.

Mother Keeper’s response played more in his head than anywhere discernible in the cave: “To know your plight. To see what you choose not to.”

Staring at the floor, hands slipping on slick rock, he cried out, “What do I not see? What more can you ask of me?”

“Sacrifice.”

“I have\! I have done as I was told\! Ophelia… She was the one who decided to come here\! I did my job\! I am *doing* my job\! *My* end of the bargain\!”

“Feckless boy.” Mother Keeper’s dismissive tone drummed in his mind. “Leave her and be done with it.”

His eyes darted to the pool. “It’s done, then? You will end the blight?”

Acheiropoita thought to wound the boy further. She thought to tell him the truth: Whether she accepted the gift or not, the result would be the same. The transmuted girl was dead, and the blight would end. But, as she knew, the two events would not be connected. 

“You will end the growing rot?” Yori begged. “Great Mother, you would make things right?”

“Make things right? Right? Ignorance, walker. You walk blighted by ignorance. Those meddlers propagate seeds sown by inattention. You stride outside the path. There is no sacrifice through The Beyond Pool. The gift is taken.”

“But it is received? Isn’t it\!?” the boy quaked. 

“Yes. Now stride forward. As tramped your kin. Self-see the gulf between consideration and appreciation.”

He did not understand the meaning of the jumbled words. His mind was still wounded by the horror of what had occurred. He had journeyed so far, and now, at the precipice of his mission, he saw only discord. Around he looked. At the disarray of objects: the carved cavern walls, the eyes of the languid god, his father’s crooked head. All danced in a spinning green mania. 

Once more he turned to the anchor. He ambled the short distance back to it, legs heavier than iron, wrested the stock onto his back, and dragged it into a pit near the pool. 

*We tread through terrible dreams, sister. Thank you.*

He took the free end of the rope and tied it securely around his left ankle. 

“This,” he pointed to the knot in full view of Mother Keeper, “you will allow me?”

“Do it and be gone.”

“Thank you,” he said, bowing out of terrified respect.

Gathering himself up, he climbed onto the rough edge of the pool. He halted at its rim. The roiling liquid below hissed at his feet. Rotten vapors floated up to greet him. He could feel a venom below him, around him, within him. Down it crawled into his central being, through his arteries, to his aching heart. A piece of him rotated around to look at the colossal god as it carefully placed his father’s head into the pit with the anchor. Yori managed to spit out, “I see,” then slipped on the rock and slid headfirst into the green pool.

Old-man Palinkos hadn’t the heart to make the trek home. Slouched on his stool, he had spent the day catchlessly casting his line into the sea. His rear ached from the immobility, and the intermittent splash lathered him in a layer of salt. Now, the sun ran against the horizon, hurried in its passion, as twilight waited eagerly beyond. 

A gull drifted about overhead, and he motioned to the bird with a piece of bread left from his uneaten lunch. The bird squawked in dismay but circled closer all the same. 

“Somebody should eat it, bird. And you’ve pestered me long enough. Come, dine on my misery.”

Down it swooped, landing a few paces from Palinkos. 

“Smarter than you look,” he proffered, tossing the bread to the gull.

As the bird claimed its prize, Palinkos waited for the last drops of sun, preparing himself for the climb home and for the scene he’d have to describe once he got there. 

He made to move across the slate. He hooked his left arm around his stool and grasped the rod with his right hand. Over the Salt Mistress’ rear hump he climbed onto the beach below, when he noticed the salt holes, curiously dry and coated in a viscous brine. 

“Wet pocks throughout the day—dry by impending night. What do you make of that?” Palinkos wondered, vaguely speaking to the gull which, he realized, had taken off. There in the water, just discernible in the fading sun, careening against the dock foundations, he saw a body.

Sonja had always disliked splitfish. The rotund fish made for good eating, yes, but the animal buried its fatty meat under a layer of razorous scales. Removing them was no simple matter. Scraping off the scales caused them to splinter into finer and finer pieces of fishy shard. The tiny pieces were impossible to get out once burrowed in her hands. Normally, Sonja would cut the scales away using a knife designed specifically for the task. But not tonight. She had been unable to find her prized knife after her son brought home a crate of splitfish for dinner. After half an hour of rummaging through her cabinets, she’d given up the hunt. Her hands—and those of her son—would just have to bear the barbs, she resolved. 

Mesteos had returned from the catch early in the afternoon. Against her better judgment, she immediately told him the news about the boy. 

“An anchor the size of *two* men?” he’d asked. Sonja barely believed it herself. For half a moment she had considered whether she’d dreamt up the entire memory. 

Of course, Mesteos had rallied the other fishermen. After their midday rests, they’d crawled back onto the boats and circled the bay looking for the boy. For hours, they searched the shallows for a tool designed to sink. Sonja recognized the futility but had not said a word. *Give them something honorable to do*, she’d thought.

The fishermen had returned shortly after sunset with empty nets. Now, Mesteos sat opposite her at the kitchen table, both their heads craned over hands covered in scales.

“I don’t understand it,” she sighed. “What could have led Yori onto the water?”

By now, they’d determined the child’s name; Mesteos knew the farm kids better than her and had never, unlike her, recoiled against their eccentricities. *Such strife*, she mulled*, and for what?* On another day, she might have blamed the boy’s parents and their gods. That faith had obviously led them astray before. But today, she had no enmity to give. The suffering in the boy’s face that morning had exhausted her sense of moral clarity. If anything, the day had left her questioning her own beliefs. *Could the Open Palm drive Mesteos down to the docks with such an anchor on his back?* *Could they kill my boy?* 

Her son worked diligently at the fish as he ignored her question. “Papa said he set out on a dinghy. A dinghy? Why go through all that trouble only to drown yourself?”

“Teos, I wish you wouldn’t say that. Or at least… Chhhah\!” A scale had dug into her thumb. She tried to teeth it out. “At least don’t speak so crassly,” she mumbled with half her thumb in her mouth. 

He looked up to her rueful glare. “Sorry, I didn’t mean to be insensitive. Papa just said that…”

“Where *is* he? Did he not come up with the rest of you?”

At that moment, the house’s flimsy back door burst open.

“Have you been on that rock all…” Sonja caught herself short. “Oh. Stefan, goodness, it’s you. Come in.” The giant man barely reigned himself in from his abrupt entrance before he doubled over in a violent coughing fit. She made for the water basin to fetch him a cup, but Stefan waved her off as he fought to compose himself. He looked dreadful, Sonja realized; practically caked in black ash. He smelt of decay. She could tell by his eyes that he hadn’t slept. 

“What are you doing here?” Mesteos asked tentatively.

“I, ahhk,” he hacked, “I…”

“Sit down, please,” Sonja said as she dragged a crude wooden stool beneath him. She was glad to see him accept it.

“This has to do with your wife’s brother, doesn’t it?” Sonja asked.

He suddenly gained life and grabbed her arm. “You spoke to him\!?” he exclaimed.

“I saw him. This morning, I did. Briefly.” Sonja put a hand over his and tried not to flinch as she pricked an embedded scale. “Teos, fetch Stefan some water, please.” They sat quietly as her son filled a cup from the basin and brought it over to the table. Stefan seemed to remember a terrible thirst and downed the cup before returning his intensity to Sonja. His eyes beckoned her to continue. 

“I saw him dragging a massive anchor through the village, headed down to the dock. I didn’t recognize him at first. I didn’t manage to stop him. Then, as Mesteos heard, he loaded the thing onto a small boat and rowed straight out to sea. Can you tell us what is going on? What would drive the boy to do such a thing?”

“And Ophelia? What about Ophelia? Was she with him?” The desperation in Stefan’s voice thickened his words. 

“Ophelia? I… haven’t heard anything about her. She wasn’t with Yori this morning.” Sonja exchanged a look with her son, who mirrored her own wary look. “Why do you ask about her? Is your wife okay?”

“One of the fishermen said that Palinkos had seen her. That’s why I came here\! If Palinkos saw her…” Stefan trailed off.

“He hasn’t come home yet,” Sonja said calmly. “It’s near dark, though, so I imagine he’ll return soon. As we wait, you can inform us of what has happened. The whole village is hungry for news.”

Whatever lucidity had gripped Stefan a moment earlier fizzled as he removed his arm from Sonja’s grasp. Words wandered out of his mouth: “The blight, Goodwoman. It had returned. We spent all night trying to light the fires. They were planning, Ophelia and Yori. Planning… nonsense. I figured if we lit the offerings before daybreak, if we just lit those forsaken trees, Ophelia would… see. It was her land—her trees.”

Mesteos, now rapt, cut into Stefan’s rant. “You were burning the orchard? Is that what you mean, Stefan? Why didn’t you tell us?”

“Yes, the orchard. The blight had sunk its teeth into the roots.” He covered his face with a hand, arm perched on the table. “We thought we could stop it before it reached the village. The omens were in our favor. Our prayers rang true. But the offering didn’t… It didn’t work. It wasn’t going to work. The Open Palm discarded us, and she knew it. Ophelia got it in her head that they should go the way of their father. She thought the Matron would stop the blight again, but I didn’t know what she planned, what it would require…”

Sonja did not have time to process his words before the back door again hammered open. This time there was no mistaking who entered her home: The boy screamed like a wounded animal as her husband practically threw him through the door.

Palinkos had not considered what would happen after he deposited Yori into his home. Hauling the sodden, despondent youth up the stairs was all he’d imagined doing that evening. That’s as far as he’d planned. Still, the retired fisherman was surprised by the scene he found in their cramped kitchen. 

As he stumbled into the room behind Yori, Palinkos caught a flicker of the atmosphere he had interrupted. His wife and son appeared to be questioning the foreman of the orchard who, in Palinkos’ estimation, looked almost as distraught as the boy now writhing on the floor. He could smell the distress wafting around the kitchen table. Like tar and sweat. Sensing trouble, Palinkos had every inclination to walk right back out the door. 

Then the foreman moved. He shot off his stool to grab Yori around the armpits. The boy’s shrieks only grew shriller as the foreman, a hulking man, hoisted him in the air and crashed him down onto the table, the force of which sent kitchenware and fish scales exploding around the room. 

The assailant bellowed, “WHERE IS SHE, YORI?”

From that moment, Palinkos lost the thread of events. The noise of the foreman’s yelling joined in with the still inconsolable boy *and* the growing protestations of his wife and son. The discord drowned out his ability to think. He watched as the man pinned Yori against the wood, then as he squeezed the boy’s face between his fingers. Mesteos tried to pull the foreman back but was easily shoved to the side, crumpling into a chair and then onto the floor. Palinkos saw his wife’s open mouth begging the foreman to stop. And he spotted the boy’s arms flailing to the side in desperate grasps. 

For all he idly witnessed, Palinkos certainly took notice when the boy managed to grab a waiting splitfish from the table and rake the fish across the bearded cheek of the man atop him. The impact managed to freeze the room momentarily; then two new sounds joined the fray: the howling of the foreman and Palinkos’ own manic laughter.

The only solution Sonja could come up with was to ply her company with drink, herself included. She forced a cocktail of papaver and raki down the boy’s throat to calm him, then divided the remaining raki between them. Her husband sat in the corner, busying himself with drink. Mesteos straddled Stefan with a pair of iron tweezers in his hands to remove specks of scale that had lodged into the man’s hair and skin. A candle, placed nearby to help the operation, lit the foremen in profile, adding to the severity of his wounds. The foreman winced often but did not complain—though he certainly appeared glad to temper the pain with liquor. 

Yori remained on the table. She gave him enough papaver to comfort but not tranquilize him completely. Once he quieted—and once she managed to force some bread and cheese into everyone’s stomachs—Sonja hesitantly led the men into conversation.

“Shall we try this again?” she broached, looking particularly at Stefan, who meekly nodded. “We’re all eager to hear the boy’s story. Yori, can you tell us what happened with you and your sister?”

The boy lay supine with his eyes closed, his hands clasped as if in prayer. The first words from him stumbled out incoherently until he found his voice: “I thought she was going to take the… star. She dug around inside me, looking for it. She did. She found it. And I thought she meant to steal it. But she left it inside me. And now it wants out. Out, out, out.”

Sonja stepped in before any of the men could speak: “Yori, dear, are you talking about your sister? Did Ophelia try to take something from you?”

“No, no. The Mother,” Yori prattled. “She saw the star. Or maybe she showed it to me, and now it’s so heavy. Goodwoman, can you help me? Can you help me get it out?”

This concerned her, but she pressed on: “Maybe later I can help you with that. But now, we need to know about Ophelia. Is she in trouble? Did she go out to sea with you?”

“Yes, yes, yes, she took me out there. Or I took her out there. We did it together.”

Stefan began to rise in anticipation, but Mesteos grabbed his shoulder and ushered him back into his chair.

“And where is she now? Did she come back with you?”

“I… I don’t… She became… She was,” Yori muttered, once again lost.

“Please, Yori,” Stefan compelled, “Just tell us where she is.”

“The Mother kept her.” The boy’s face contorted as he spoke. “She’s with the monster—with father. She felt there was no other way.”

Though she was certain Stefan knew already, Sonja saw how the confirmation crushed him. He took his scarred face out of the light and stood facing the corner opposite Palinkos. His muffled cries didn’t stop the boy from continuing.

“That day at the pyres, she told me she knew what father had not. She knew why he’d never returned. We needed to give the god a token. No, not a token. A worthy gift. That’s what father had forgotten. He’d gone alone. I saw him. I saw him entering the cave in my dream. So many dreams. But she thought she could get us there. Together. She’d been learning how to do it. Transmutations, yes. Simple transmutations. What the priestdom practiced.”

“The priestdom?” Sonja inquired.

“They practiced on the wood,” Yori continued. “They wanted to change it. Change it into something else. That’s where Ophelia got the idea.”

“The anchor,” Palinkos muttered, already well on his way to being drunk. “That’s foul, foul stuff, Stefan. What happened? What made the priestdom try *that*?”

The foreman rocked himself in the corner. Between sucks of air, he managed to get out, “The blight. We thought it might… It could be… an option. If we sacrificed the trees, we could transmute the poison into another form that would appease the Open Palm. But the pyres didn’t go.”

Palinkos grimaced at the man: “And the girl took matters into her own hands?” 

“I thought she would come back,” Yori whined. “She said we would come back.” 

That was enough for Sonja. She laid a hand on the boy’s breast and spoke warmly, to him and the others: “It’s okay, Yori. That’s more than enough for now. I think you should get some rest.” She motioned with her head for Mesteos to take the boy into the other room, and he did.

As she got up to follow, she spotted Stefan making for the door: “Stefan, stop. Please don’t leave. There’s nothing out there for you right now.”

The man froze. His frame filled the doorway. Facing away from her, he asked, “Why didn’t I say that?”

The question caught her off guard. “What do you mean? Say what?”

“Please don’t leave,” he wept. “That’s all I had to say.”

Sonja tried to summon a reply but could not find the words. She simply stood behind the foreman as a frail shadow, willing him to return to the table through psychic goodwill.

A shout interrupted their détente. “Mother, come quick\!” her son called. Sonja swung around, and as she did, she heard the door slam shut behind her. *Let him go*, she forced herself to think as she shuffled through their home’s meager furniture.

In the bedroom, Yori hacked over a bucket held by a panicked Mesteos.

“He just started doing this\! What should we do?” her son asked.

Palinkos joined them in the already cramped room. Drunkenly, he offered, “It’s the sea spirit. He mentioned it, remember? Caught in his belly.”

Sonja knelt next to the child and spoke kind words as she rubbed his back. For too long he struggled with something in his throat until, finally, he retched it free. Then, seemingly contented, Yori rolled onto his side and tucked himself into a loose knot.

Inside the bucket lay a tiny, wet orb. It gave off a dim light that churned around itself ever so slightly, like waves lapping over waves. 

“What is it?” asked Mesteos.

“A gift,” chuckled his father.

Sonja plucked the orb from the bucket and wiped away the saliva with a rag. Rising, she held the starfall between them. 

“Should you be holding it?”  her son. 

Sonja watched the boy on her bed. The slight rise and fall of his chest. She was scared for him. Bad memories awaited him when he woke. The morning would not be kind—to him or to any one of them. She’d have to go visit his mother and tell her all about these dreadful events. *Not everything, though*, she thought. That woman would have enough to deal with. She didn’t need to recount every moment in detail. This moment, for example—she certainly wouldn’t tell the woman what she saw now. The boy’s face had curled into a calm smile. Whatever he dreamt now was best left to him alone.

Ignoring her own son, she walked past her family and into her garden. It was obvious—she felt—what she needed to do. Her joints ached as she lowered herself down. She breathed in to compose herself as she nestled into the dirt. Then she worked the soil. The starfall gave her just enough light to make out what she was doing. Carefully, she used a small trowel to dig a hole in the soil near the apricot and situated the starfall in its new home. 

The tree would be the center of the garden, she hoped. Her grandchildren would lounge under its shade and pluck fruit from its branches. 

Mesteos’ voice drifted up behind her: “Mother, are you alright?”
